How a forecast is scored

A Pyrecast forecast says when fire will reach each patch of ground. We check that against where the fire really went, counting only ground that burned after the forecast was made. Here is one made-up fire, step by step.

1The timeline

  1. Monday 15:40: firefighters map the fire's perimeter. It's the latest one before the forecast, so it's our before picture.
  2. Tuesday 07:04: a satellite passes over and detects heat. Pyrecast starts its forecast right then, from the perimeter plus those satellite detections, which reach beyond the perimeter where the fire has grown since Monday.
  3. We score the forecast at fixed times after it starts. This example uses 12 hours, so the window runs from 07:04 to 19:04. (Real runs are also scored at 24 hours and 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 10 and 14 days.)
  4. Tuesday 18:10: a new perimeter is mapped, 0.9 hours before the window ends. It's our after picture. A score needs a perimeter mapped within 3 hours of the window's end.
  5. Satellite passes at 13:25 and 14:05 detect heat during the window: a second, separate check.

2Four maps of the same ground

The forecast works on a grid of small squares (30 m across in reality). To keep it readable, each square here stands for 10 acres.

ABefore the forecast

Gray is inside Monday's perimeter. Hatched is where the forecast started burning beyond it, from the 07:04 satellite detections (dots). Together they're burned before: this ground can't count for or against the forecast.

BThe forecast

When the forecast expects fire to arrive. Squares it expects within 12 hours (dark purple) are its prediction for this window. Later arrivals don't count at 12 hours.

CWhat happened

Tuesday 18:10's perimeter (red line) and the ground that burned beyond what had burned before (pink). Dots are the satellite detections during the window.

DThe score

Lay B over C. Each square of new ground is a hit (predicted and burned), a false alarm (predicted, didn't burn) or a miss (burned, not predicted).

Why the forecast's own start counts as burned

Pyrecast treats the hatched squares as already burning when it starts, so it never predicts fire arriving there. If we only removed Monday's perimeter, those squares would count as fire the forecast missed, though it was never trying to predict them.

4The rest of the rules

  • Five versions per run. Each forecast comes in five weather scenarios (the 10th to 90th percentile). Each is scored on its own.
  • Many horizons. Every run is scored at 12 and 24 hours and 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 10 and 14 days, each against the perimeter mapped closest to that window's end.
  • Perimeter times. A perimeter is timed by when it was mapped. When that time is missing or doesn't look right, we use when it was last edited instead, and record which.
  • What's shown by default. A score appears in the map and the report's default view only if the perimeter was mapped within 3 hours of the window's end, at least 10 acres burned that weren't burned before, and no more than 10% of that growth fell off the edge of the forecast's map.
  • When the forecast's start wasn't saved. For about 8% of runs, our archive missed the forecast's first hour. Then every satellite detection up to the run stands in for its start.
  • Why hotspots are only a side check. Satellites pass a few times a day and can't see through thick smoke or cloud, so missing detections don't mean the ground didn't burn. Hotspots feed the capture check only, never precision.
  • Same grid throughout. The perimeters and detections are laid onto the forecast's own grid, and every area above is a count of its squares.
